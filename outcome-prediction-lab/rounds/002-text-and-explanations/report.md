# Round 002 report — text on codes and drug names, and self-explanatory patient narratives

**Status:** complete. **Pre-registration:** [`README.md`](./README.md). **Run entirely through the
`outcome-prediction-lab` CLI:** configs in [`configs/`](./configs/), results in [`runs/`](./runs/),
table in [`comparison.md`](./comparison.md). **Test-panel looks this round: none** — no text variant
met the pre-registered bar (≥ +0.003 CV AUROC over the reference).

Label throughout: **non-obstetric admission** (O47), so pregnancy codes can't inflate text gains.

## 1. Headline

- **TF-IDF on plain-language code descriptions adds nothing for gradient boosting** (CV AUROC 0.7979
  vs 0.7988 for code tokens), but **helps a linear model** (sparse logistic regression 0.7958 → 0.7979):
  related codes share words in their names ("heart", "chronic", "kidney"), so a model that can't learn
  interactions on its own gets them for free.
- **Character n-grams are the right tool for messy drug names**: mapping pharmacy-recorded names
  first seen in 2021–2022 to their therapeutic class, character n-grams get **72.6%** right (82.5% at
  the top class level) against **68.4%** for words — and leave no name without a match, while words
  find nothing in common for 12% of names. As *model features*, though, drug-name n-grams add nothing
  over drug-class tokens (0.7982 vs 0.7988).
- **Every patient now gets a self-explanatory narrative**: plain words, full names instead of codes,
  and observed rates for meaningful groups of real adults (§4). `explain --all` writes one for every
  test-panel patient.
- **A round-001 data defect was found and fixed**: drug classes used only the third level of the
  Multum hierarchy, leaving 34% of fills (e.g. lisinopril, omeprazole) without a class. Fixed;
  measured effect on prediction: none (CV 0.7993 → 0.7988, noise). Round 001's numbers stay
  reproducible (its script pins the old behaviour).

columns for 44,000 people).

## 3. Drug-name normalisation (`drug_names.json`)

Train on 4,904 distinct pharmacy names (2016–2020); test on 656 names first seen in 2021–2022.

| Method | Class right | Top-level class right | Names with nothing in common |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact lookup | 0% (by construction; 97% on the 1,549 names already seen) | — | — |
| Word TF-IDF, nearest neighbour | 68.4% | 77.4% | 11.9% |
| **Character 3–5-gram TF-IDF, nearest neighbour** | **72.6%** | **82.5%** | **0%** |

Why characters win: abbreviations share letters, not words — `ADAPLENS-BNZYL PEROX` matches
`ADAPALENE` on character pieces. Where they fail is instructive too: `ADHANSIA XR` (a stimulant)
matched `LARISSIA` (a contraceptive) on spelling alone — similarity of letters isn't similarity of
medicine, which is why production mapping should use RxNorm codes when FHIR provides them, and
treat n-gram matches as suggestions for review.

## 4. Self-explanatory patient narratives

Generated by `outcome-prediction-lab explain` (examples: [`explanations/ref-codes-lsa-examples.md`](./explanations/ref-codes-lsa-examples.md)).
Rules:
- **Plain words, full names**: every code is replaced by its name — ICD-10 categories and CCSR
  groups from AHRQ's own MEPS documentation, gaps from the CDC/NCHS ICD-10-CM release, Multum drug
  classes from AHRQ's therapeutic-class table. Labels AHRQ truncated end in "…".
- **Meaningful aggregates**: the estimate is compared with all adults, and with adults of the same
  age band and sex; each reason quotes the *observed* next-year rate among comparable adults in the
  training panels ("28% of adults with a hospital stay in the past year were hospitalised again").
- **The marginal-vs-conditional trap is named**: when a factor lowers the estimate although its group
  is above average on its own (statins, blood-pressure medicines), the sentence says so. A plausible
  reading is a condition that is being managed — but the model shows *what* it weighed, not *why*.
- **Deliberately not narrated**: race/ethnicity (reported in aggregate on the model card instead), and
  the patient's actual outcome (an explanation is not a performance look).
- Templated from numbers, never written by a language model.

> This 56-year-old man has an estimated 27% chance of an overnight hospital stay that isn't related
> to pregnancy or childbirth in the next 12 months — about 3.6 times the average adult (7.4%), and
> about 3.6 times men aged 45–64 (7.5%). That puts him above 95% of adults. The estimate is pushed up
> mainly by taking a medicine of the class “calcium channel blocking agents” (17% of adults with this
> were hospitalised for reasons other than childbirth); 7 serious long-term conditions on the
> Elixhauser comorbidity list (29% of adults with 5 or more such conditions were hospitalised for
> reasons other than childbirth); and 3 emergency-room visits in the past year (16% of adults with an
> ER visit were hospitalised for reasons other than childbirth the following year). It is held down
> by taking a medicine of the class “HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors” (15% of adults with this were
> hospitalised for reasons other than childbirth — above the average on its own, but for someone with
> the rest of this patient’s record it points to lower risk) … This is a planning estimate from
> patterns in national survey data: it shows what the model weighed, not what causes illness, and it
> is not a diagnosis.

## 6. Limitations and open items

- The scorecard's person-scored criteria (measurability in customer data, actionability, lead time,
  FHIR representability, regulatory posture) are **owed by a person** — pass them as `--criteria`.
- Comparison rates come from MEPS survey adults (2016–2021), not a customer population.
- Narratives explain the reference model; with LSA, code-level reasons are an approximation (linear
  attribution through the SVD), labelled as such in code and docs.
- MEPS labels for some CCSR groups are abbreviated by AHRQ ("Oth", "conds", "&").
